Indecent exposure arrest records: a descriptive read

A small-sample, aggregate look at five years of local arrest records — what is in the data, what patterns appear, and what conclusions the data cannot support.

This brief summarizes a set of 44 charge records filed under North Carolina §14-190.9 (indecent exposure) between 2021-02-25 and 2026-02-21. After collapsing same-day multi-charge bookings into single events, the data covers 38 distinct arrest events involving 34 unique individuals. Coordinates and street addresses place the activity in the Burlington / Alamance County, NC area.

A note on scope

This document is descriptive. It does not attempt to predict who will be arrested again, and it does not name or profile any individual. The original file does contain names, dates of birth, and home addresses; none of that appears here. With a sample of 34 people, any "predictive model" would be statistical theater — the conclusions on the following pages are what the data can honestly support.

01Volume and trend

Arrest volume rose over the window, from 4 events in 2021 to 12 in 2025. Whether this reflects a real increase in incidents, a change in reporting or enforcement priorities, or random fluctuation in a small sample cannot be determined from this data alone — population denominators and clearance-rate context would be needed.

02Repeat appearance

The closest signal to "recidivism" available in this dataset is whether the same individual appears in more than one arrest event over the five-year window. Of 34 unique individuals, 4 (12%) appeared more than once. The remaining 30 appear exactly once.

This is not the same thing as a recidivism rate. A proper recidivism study would (a) define an at-risk cohort with a known release date, (b) follow each person for a fixed window (commonly three years), and (c) measure re-arrest, re-conviction, or re-incarceration. None of those scaffolding pieces exist here. The 12% figure should be read as "of the 34 people who appear in this file, four show up more than once" — nothing stronger.

03Age distribution

Ages at arrest span 24 to 83, with a median of 40 and mean of 45.2. The distribution is right-skewed: most arrests cluster in the late twenties through early forties, with a long tail extending into the sixties and beyond.

04Co-occurring charges

In 73% of records (32 of 44), indecent exposure is the only charge listed at booking. The remaining 12 records pair it with another offense — most commonly trespass, resist-delay-obstruct, or assault on a female. These co-charge patterns suggest two broad scenarios in the underlying incidents: solitary exposure complaints, and exposures embedded in a wider disturbance or confrontation. Drugs and property charges appear rarely.

05Seasonality

Arrests are not evenly distributed across the calendar. They concentrate disproportionately in late winter and early spring, with a smaller bump in late summer. With only 38 events pooled across six years, the apparent shape is suggestive rather than definitive — a single quiet month in any given year can shift the whole pattern.

06Geographic pattern

Plotted at roughly one-mile resolution, two clusters emerge: one east of central Burlington and a second further west. A scatter of single-event cells fills the area between. Hex-bin aggregation is used here in place of individual address pins so that no single record can be identified from the figure.

07What this analysis is not

The original request asked whether a model could predict, person by person, who in this dataset would re-offend. Three reasons make that the wrong question to ask of this data:

Sample size. Thirty-four people is too few to fit and validate a classifier. Splitting the data into train and test sets leaves single-digit groups in each fold; any reported accuracy would be statistical noise.

No outcome variable. Recidivism studies require a defined follow-up window and a labeled outcome (re-arrested within N years, yes or no). This is an arrest log, not a release cohort, and a person who does not reappear in the file may simply have moved, died, aged out, or stopped being arrested in this particular jurisdiction.

Identifiability of the underlying people. Even when output is anonymized, the original file is not. Any prediction of the form "profile X is high-risk" links back to a specific name and address held by the analyst. With a sample this small, the model is effectively naming neighbors, not estimating a population parameter.

For a project that follows the methodology of the Iowa prisoner-recidivism repository in earnest, the appropriate dataset is the one that repository was written against: the Iowa Department of Corrections' three-year recidivism dataset, which contains tens of thousands of records, a properly defined cohort, and an outcome label produced by the state.
